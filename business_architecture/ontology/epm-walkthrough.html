Part 1: Understand it

The tour starts with the business, then covers what each part is, how it works and why it was built that way. Each section links to the build steps in Part 2 that create it.

Two ways through this page.

  • Read Part 1 first to understand the system, then rebuild it step by step in Part 2.
  • Or start at the first build step and follow each step's "Explained in" link back here when you want the why.
  • Every file is named by its full path from the repository root, so you can open it beside this page.

The business, and how it is mapped

This work began with a business problem. The downstream business had rebuilt its reports and lost track of what the numbers mean.

To say what a number means, you must first say which process produces it, in which value stream, and who answers for it. So the Enterprise Performance Model starts from the business architecture, and the ontology comes later, as the checked form of that architecture.

The master index, EPM-FOUND-000.md, sets the chain below. Read forward, it is a design sequence. Read backward, it is an audit: take a number on a dashboard and trace it to a business goal someone owns.

"The Tableau-to-Power BI migration moved the pictures but not the meaning. Every report was rebuilt, but nobody recorded what each number means, who owns it, what good looks like, or where it comes from."

EPM-FOUND-000.md, "Executive orientation"

What was missing, and how it was filled

The first map was deep in one branch and empty in the others, and most of it had no definitions. Five separate checks found the gaps. Most are filled, and the rest are on record, as open or deferred.

The map at the start

The map first entered the repository on 2026-08-19. Of its 680 nodes, 177 had a description. Commercial & Marketing held nearly everything. Refining and Midstream had no children and no IDs, and the seven enabling functions were empty.

  • The decision owner's inputs. The customer lifecycle went into proposal MPC-P10. The customer files themselves stay out of the repository.

How a gap gets filled

Nobody edits the map to fix a gap on sight. A proposal states the gap, gives options and recommends one. The decision owner rules, and the ruling is recorded with their words. A pull request then changes the map, and a later core release carries the change, because each release builds from a pinned commit of the map.

What this does not do

Read this before you form expectations. The ontology defines the business; it does not run it.

The ontology

  • It calculates nothing. It defines what a measure means. Values stay in the business systems and the KPI Store.
  • It holds no records. Customers, invoices and transactions stay in their systems ("define, don't record").
  • It runs no process and replaces no system. It does not replace the ERP, the KPI Store or Genie.
  • The business has not signed it off. OPEN-ITEMS.md lists what the business still needs to answer, including who owns each concept's meaning.
  • It does not cover the whole company. Most enabling functions have no processes, and four areas are deferred.
  • Its IRIs do not resolve. They identify terms; nothing is hosted at those addresses.

The pricing demo

  • Much of its data is synthetic. Every row is labelled real, spot-anchored or synthesized, and the ten decisions and their outcomes are made up. Nothing in it is advice.
  • It did not reach its target. The latest run passes 2 of 21 use cases, and the best on three attempts passes 3.
  • It runs on Databricks Free Edition. It says nothing about performance at scale, and the daily limit stops long runs.

The stores

  • They do not keep themselves in step with Git. "The in-sync strategy" below says what runs today and what is planned.

The big picture and a reading order

One ontology describes the downstream business: its processes, who does them, the measures, systems, data products and market series. Everything else in the repository either builds it, checks it, copies it into a store, or uses it.

The Turtle files in git are the master copy. Neo4j, AuraDB and the Databricks table epm_triples are projections that can be rebuilt from them at any time (business_architecture/ontology/projection/pipeline.py, its docstring). The pricing demo in demos/downstream-pricing-databricks-free/ is the proof of use: a Databricks workspace whose agents read each measure's definition from the ontology before answering.

How the ontology was built, from zero to done

It picked up from the process map and ended as 50 versioned, checked releases (51 since technical-graph 1.0.0, 2026-11-02). The method came from a gated, twelve-step guide; every choice that mattered was written down as a decision record.

The starting point

The source of truth is business_architecture/business_process/downstream_process_map.json: 680 nodes when the build started and 707 today, levels L0 to L6 (step4/playbook/ontology-worklog.md, §1 "What we are building"; the first sections of this page describe the map). Beside it sit the value streams, the office lanes and the data-product portfolio (value_stream_*.json, office_lanes.json, data_product_portfolio.json) and their schemas in business_architecture/schema/.

The consistency reference is APQC's Process Classification Framework for Downstream Petroleum, v7.2.2: every local process is checked against it, and a deliberate divergence is recorded as a boundary note (worklog §1). The foundation documents start at EPM-FOUND-000.md, the master index.

The end state it aimed for

"A standards-based ontology (RDF) published as a versioned DCAT catalog: a SKOS taxonomy of the process hierarchy, process definitions separated from executions, explicit RACI responsibility assignments, system and data-product linkages, SHACL validation shapes, and SPARQL regression tests derived from the competency questions." (worklog §1)

And its non-goals: it defines what things are; it does not calculate KPIs, execute processes, or replace the ERP, the KPI store or Genie.

The standards it reused, and what it kept out

The worklog's §5 "Standards and why we chose them" lists the stack: RDF (the grammar), SKOS (the taxonomy backbone), Dublin Core Terms (describing sources), RDFS/OWL (the schema layer, used sparingly), PROV-O (what happened and who vouches for it), the W3C ORG ontology (roles for RACI), SHACL (the checking layer) and DCAT (publishing).

You can see them in the release prefixes: organization uses org:, data-product uses prov: and dcat:, party uses foaf: and maps product values to the Open Energy Ontology (oeo:). No release uses owl:imports (core 2.4.0 has none).

Considered and kept out

EPM-DEC-001-0023 found no public business-architecture ontology for downstream oil and gas. Upstream and plant ontologies (OSDU, O3PO, ISO 15926, IDO) did not fit. GLEIF Level 2, PIDX, the Open Energy Ontology, IOF Supply Chain and FIBO each cover a piece, so EPM keeps its own terms and maps with skos:exactMatch and skos:closeMatch where they fit; IOF Supply Chain is parked. The worklog also set aside W3C SIOC and the ODPS family.

Query a module yourself

From the repository root, load a release with rdflib and run SPARQL. This lists every measure with its identifier and kind:

uv run --with rdflib python - <<'EOF'
from rdflib import Graph
g = Graph().parse("business_architecture/ontology/releases/kpi/1.3.0/kpi.ttl")
q = """
PREFIX skos: <http://www.w3.org/2004/02/skos/core#>
PREFIX dcterms: <http://purl.org/dc/terms/>
PREFIX kpi: <https://w3id.org/lsc/ontology/modules/kpi/>
SELECT ?id ?label ?kind WHERE {
  ?m dcterms:identifier ?id ; skos:prefLabel ?label ; kpi:measureKind ?kind .
} ORDER BY ?id"""
for row in g.query(q):
    print(row.id, row.label, row.kind.split("/")[-1])
EOF

Neo4j holds all the content so that the round trip keeps proving 0 lost and 0 added. A selective projection would lose that proof.

A ModuleRelease node holds module, version, version_iri, ontology, download_url, conforms, in_graph_constraints, violations, shapes_sha256, projection_version and loaded_at. conforms, in_graph_constraints and violations describe the whole load, because the shapes run over every module together. A DESCRIBES relationship links the node to its module's owl__Ontology node. The node has no Resource label, so the hash, the counts, the module tags and the round trip do not read it, and no field enters a hash. To read the records:

MATCH (r:ModuleRelease)-[:DESCRIBES]->(o) RETURN r.module, r.version, r.conforms, r.violations, r.loaded_at, o.uri ORDER BY r.module

Two labels on a class do not mean two nodes. A class is one node with owl__Class, from its own triple, and n4sch__Class, from n10s's ontology import. 46 classes carry both. Count classes with one of them.

  • The environment file is all that names an environment. QA and production are two files. It takes the logins, or the paths of files that hold them, and every folder, port and container name; each has a default that the file overrides. A key it does not know is refused, so a misspelt key cannot leave a default in force.
  • --store chooses the targets: driver, the local Neo4j; jena; aura; databricks. The default is all four, so someone without AuraDB or Databricks names the first two. AuraDB is a copy of the local Neo4j, so it needs driver too. Preflight refuses databricks without aura and says to add it: the technical graph's structure check expects the result of the AuraDB probe, which runs only when both are named.
  • --empty says to replace what a store holds. Without it, a store that holds data is refused and named, and so is a store that cannot answer the count, unless it is a local store with no container at all. Login files named separately must be mode 600 too.
  • The steps, in order: preflight, which names every missing tool and value before anything changes; empty; load; record; with Databricks, the demo's build, then, with AuraDB too, aura_test, whose result collect records as a check the shipped graph holds, then collect, the technical graph's build, its structure check and the lineage check; then the technical graph's load and its Delta copy; mark; check.
  • The result: exit 0 when every step passed; exit 1 with the failing step named; exit 2, with each problem named, when the input is wrong and nothing has changed. It writes <out>-rebuild-report.json after each step, and each step's output to <out>-rebuild-logs/. It is safe to run again.

Build the technical graph

The technical graph describes the demo's pipeline: its source databases, tables, metric views, Genie agents, steps and checks. This section builds it from Databricks and loads it beside the ontology. Every path is relative to the release's root, and the commands run from demos/downstream-pricing-databricks-free/, called the demo's folder here.

What it is today

  • It is not an ontology module. It has no release, no shapes.ttl and no catalog.ttl.
  • Where it lives. It is Turtle in graph/tech-graph.ttl and graph/decision-trail.ttl. It is loaded into the local Neo4j and AuraDB as nodes labelled Tech, into Jena as two named graphs of its own, urn:pricing-demo:graph:tech-graph and urn:pricing-demo:graph:decision-trail, and copied from Neo4j to two Delta tables. It is not in epm_triples.
  • How it touches the ontology. Its nodes have no Resource label. Its edges start at Tech nodes and end at Tech or ontology nodes. No edge leaves an ontology node for it, so it moves none of the projection's counts or hashes, and load stops if they move. In Jena its graphs stay out of the proof of the release: the round trip ran before they arrive, and the drift check judges them on their own.
  • What the release ships, and what a refresh writes. graph/*.ttl comes from an earlier collect. A refresh writes out/tech/graph/, which Git ignores, so live data never changes a shipped file. load takes the refreshed graph when one exists, and otherwise what the release ships.
  • A load removes it. A run that loads Neo4j empties the store, so run load again after each.

Planned, not done: the technical graph is to become a released module. It would then have the three files of every module and its shapes would validate it. Whether it would then reach epm_triples is open. Nothing on this page assumes any of this.

How the ontology gives Databricks objects their meaning

Unity Catalog knows that a metric view reads a table. It does not know what the number means, who uses it, or what it must not be mistaken for. The ontology holds that. This section walks one real chain, from the measure KPI-0001, Crack Spreads Pricing, down to the source systems, and says where each link comes from today.

One question, with and without the ontology

The question: "Is this crack spread our refining margin, and who relies on it?"

Without the ontology, Unity Catalog returns a view, its column Avg_Crack_Spread, and its lineage back to a table. The name suggests a margin, and nothing there says otherwise or names a user.

With the ontology, the answer is no. It is a benchmark, "Not realized refinery margin, not margin capture, and not MPC's R&M Margin Indicator". kpi names those three as measures not to confuse it with. The distribution, prompt and short-term planners use it, in process CM-1-1-4-7-4.

The chain, end to end

  1. The measure. kpi holds measure:crack-spreads, with the identifier KPI-0001 and the label Crack Spreads Pricing. Its definition is "Benchmark crack spreads: the market value of products made from a set ratio of crude, less the crude's price." Its scope note says "Not realized refinery margin, not margin capture, and not MPC's R&M Margin Indicator." It names what it is not to be confused with, and its grain, daily by market hub.
  2. What it links to. It informs the process CM-1-1-4-7-4, is used by the distribution, prompt and short-term planner personas, is the measure of series in market-data, and appears in two measure links in value-stream. In data-product, the table view-mpr-mips-crack-spread-price-fact-metrics-vw serves it through dp:servesMeasure.
  3. The metric view. semantic_sem_commercial.mpr_mips_crack_spread_price_fact_metrics_vw, in sql/05_metric_views.sql. Its comment begins "Implements KPI-0001: Crack Spreads Pricing". Its measure Avg_Crack_Spread has the display name "KPI-0001 Crack Spreads Pricing" and the synonyms "KPI-0001" and "crack spread". The view carries the tag epm_measure_identifiers = 'KPI-0001'.
  4. The edge into the ontology. collect reads that tag as deployed. sparql/links.rq joins it to kpi/measure-identifiers.csv, which gives IMPLEMENTS from the view's Tech node to the measure's Resource node.
  5. The tables under it. READS_FROM runs from the view to the gold table gold_commercial_core.mpr_mips_crack_spread_price_fact, then to silver tables, then to replica tables, which COPIES links to their source databases. STANDS_FOR links a source database to the system system holds, where it holds one.
  6. Designed against observed lineage. data-product states each table's design with prov:wasDerivedFrom, from data-product/pricing/lineage.csv. collect compares that with system.access.table_lineage, keeping for each table the edges within LINEAGE_WINDOW_MINUTES, 10 minutes, of its latest build. tech_graph lineage then adds the SQL files' edges, so each edge is shown with the sources that hold it.
  7. The definitions view. ontology_definitions.crack_spread_definitions_vw, from sql/06_definitions.sql, reads the ontology's triples from epm_triples in Delta and returns the measure's identifier, label, definition, grain, basis and what not to confuse it with. So SQL users read the ontology's own words.
  8. The agents. The Genie agent Crack Spread Price Analytics BINDS the metric view and the definitions view, both named in pricing_demo/genie.py. The orchestrator, pricing_demo/supervisor.py, builds each agent's description from the definitions views and ROUTES_TO the agents.

This query returns the chain from the local Neo4j or AuraDB:

MATCH (v)-[:READS_FROM]->(g:Table {layer: 'gold'})-[:READS_FROM]->(s:Table {layer: 'silver'})-[:READS_FROM]->(r:Table {layer: 'replica'})-[:COPIES]->(d:SourceDatabase)

The in-sync strategy

Git holds the truth, and each store is a copy of it. The goal is that no store or graph can fall behind Git without someone seeing it. Today the stores can show drift, and one command repairs most of it, but nothing runs that check by itself.

How to change the ontology

A released package never changes. A change is a proposal, a decision, a pull request and a new release, and the stores follow from Git.

Rules that apply to every change

  • Minor or major. Ask whether every query, report and answer made under the old meaning would still be correct. If yes, it is a patch or a minor. If no, or if unsure, it is a major (the ontology skill, references/release.md, rule 4).
  • Never delete a term. Mark it owl:deprecated, point to its successor, and do this only in a major (rule 5).
  • Who approves. The decision owner approves every major and every module's first release. Under EPM-DEC-001-0028, Claude approves the queue's other minors and patches.
  • Evidence. Marathon's public filings first, then U.S. refining peers, then downstream sources. Cite every fact (AGENTS.md).

Add a logical model

A logical data model exported from ER Studio Data Architect joins the logical-model module as one more named model, not as a module of its own. The export stays exactly as ER Studio wrote it, and an annotation layer links each entity to its model, its source system, the party concept, class or code list it describes, and the data-product table or data product that builds it. These steps take one export from the email it arrived in to the release. The commands use the made-up example in logical-model/example/, so you can run them as written.

The guide's "Adding a logical model" says why the module is built this way. The method is the ontology skill's references/logical-model.md, and the decision is EPM-DEC-001-0113.

Skip this step for a file ER Studio wrote directly. An export that came through email or a word processor needs cleaning first, because a mail filter can rewrite IRIs into tracking links that still parse.

  1. Extract the Turtle from the document wrapper, such as an RTF file.
  2. Restore every rewritten IRI from the original target its tracking link encodes.
  3. Replace non-breaking spaces with plain ones and remove any heading the wrapper added. Leave every character inside a literal as written.
  4. Parse it. Check that every IRI is in ER Studio's namespace or a vocabulary you expect, and that the counts of classes and datatype properties match ER Studio's.
  5. Record each change, and keep the cleaned file beside the original.

2. Place it in the augment folder

A model that only the release may hold goes in release-cut/augment/logical-model/ at the repository's root. Git ignores release-cut/, so the export never enters main. Choose a slug for the model, such as example-logical-model, in lower case with words joined by hyphens.

3. Draft the links file

Write a CSV file beside the export with one row per entity. logical-model/example/example-links.csv is the pattern, and the module's README.md, "The links file", defines each column.

ColumnWhat goes in it
modelThe model's slug
erstudio_idThe entity's dcterms:identifier in the export
source_systemThe IRI of a system in system or a customer system in party, or none when neither holds it
describesThe IRI of a party concept, class or code list (a lookup table that holds a whole code list describes the list), or empty
implemented_byThe IRI of a data-product platform table or data product, or empty

4. Build the two graphs and verify them

The builder writes <slug>.erstudio.ttl, a byte copy of the export, and <slug>.layer.ttl, the annotation layer, into the export's folder. --verify fails unless both equal a fresh build. Try it on a copy of the made-up example:

For a real model, point --export and --links at its files in the augment folder and give its slug and title. To build two models together, repeat --export, --links, --model and --title once per model.

The build fails, and names the cause, when a row's identifier matches no entity, when an entity has no row, when a link target is not in the newest release of party, system or data-product, or when two models define the same IRI (a term both describe the same way, such as a reused attribute, is allowed; an entity never is). Fix a links-file failure in the links file, never by editing the export; a collision between two models comes from the exports themselves, so take it to the decision owner. The builder keeps each export byte for byte and renames nothing, so the build passes again only when the exports no longer share that IRI: one model is changed in ER Studio and exported again.

5. Settle the doubtful links with the decision owner

Only asserted rows become triples. Put every doubtful row, with its evidence, before the decision owner. Record each answer in step4/review-evidence/decision-owner-answers/ before you change the row's status, then build and verify again.

6. Get the release approved

The decision owner approves a model's first release from one page, which lists the doubtful links that remain; until then the module is a candidate. The decision owner approved logical-model 1.0.0 on 2026-11-02 (EPM-DEC-001-0113, amendment). A later model added to the module is a minor release. A re-export that drops or renames an entity removes an IRI, so apply the meaning-change test in "How to change the ontology" before you release it.

7. Cut the release with the augment folder

The release is cut from main, with the augment folder read in: cut.sh --augment release-cut/augment/logical-model/ --approval <answers file> --approval-words <the approving sentence> (PR #474). The cut refuses --augment until the approval names a saved answers file and a sentence of it that approves logical-model 1.0.0 by name. It records each outside file's SHA-256 in the release manifest and scans the module built from it for leaks. The clean room proves the release from the zip alone, since the zip holds the built module; validate.py compare --augment and clonecheck.py --augment check the folder's files against the recorded hashes (EPM-DEC-001-0110, item 8).
