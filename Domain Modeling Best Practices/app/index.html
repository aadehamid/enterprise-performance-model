3.3 Why “Customer” is not one object

“Customer” is a contextual business role, not automatically one universal enterprise object. A legal organization, commercial account, invoice recipient, payment-responsible entity, delivery location, contract party, credit counterparty, tax-liable party, and title-transfer party can be related but distinct — and the same party may hold several of these roles simultaneously, while different roles in one transaction may be held by different legal entities in a corporate hierarchy.

Party / legal entity
  → customer account
  → party role in a commercial, financial, logistics, or operational context
  → participation in a specific order, ticket, invoice, or payment

A repeated “customer” value across two systems suggests, but does not prove, identity equivalence, role equivalence, title ownership, tax responsibility, or financial responsibility. This is exactly the gap the mandate’s “Domain concept perspective” asks us to close by translating Universal Data Model (UDM) artifacts — the well-established Party Model pattern, in which “Party” is a supertype of Person and Organization, and every business relationship (customer, carrier, guarantor, payer) is a separate, time-bounded role a Party can hold, rather than a permanent subtype of “Customer” (Silverston, The Data Model Resource Book; worked party-relationship examples).

This pattern is not academic - it is already the design basis of the ERP in scope. SAP's Business Partner model, which underlies S/4HANA Sales & Distribution, formally separates the legal party (the Business Partner) from the partner functions it performs in a transaction: Sold-to, Bill-to, and Payer are distinct, independently assignable party roles. Ship-to in that model is a location (o2c:ShipTo / o2c:Site), not a party role. One legal customer frequently does not fill every party role in a real sale (SAP Business Partner structure; SAP Partner Functions documentation; technical detail in §17). Our job is to generalize that pattern across every system in §3.1 - not just inside the ERP.

3.4 Why it matters

  • Slower onboarding. Manual reconciliation across systems slows customer onboarding and adds operational overhead every time a new commercial relationship is set up.
  • Billing, reporting, and compliance risk. Conflicting or duplicate records create downstream risk wherever customer data is used — billing, regulatory reporting, and credit decisions among them.
  • No shared vocabulary. Business units lack a common definition of “customer,” slowing any future MDM investment and causing the same argument to recur in different rooms.
  • Risk compounds over time. Every new system added without a common definition makes the problem harder to unwind later.

PART A — CONTEXT AND MANDATE4. Scope boundary for this phase

4.1 In scope IN SCOPE

  • Discovery & Inventory — identifying source systems and objects; inventorying current-state Customer/Party representations (Process-Lifecycle items 1–2; §6–§7).
  • Conceptual & Logical Modeling — defining the conceptual model (entities and relationships) and the logical model (attributes, keys, cardinality) (items 3–4; §8–§9).
  • Standardization & Governance — cross-system mapping and standardization; producing the Domain Modeling Playbook and governance rules (items 5–6; §10–§11).

4.2 Explicitly out of scope OUT OF SCOPE

  • Full MDM execution: running matching, merging, survivorship, golden-record creation, publication, and monitoring (the kind SAP MDG or Profisee would run). Those appear in this document only as the shape our outputs must feed later (§12, §14). This phase does not auto-link, does not operate a match queue, and does not persist production match decisions.

The primary goal of this phase is a single, standardized conceptual and logical view of the Customer object across Salesforce, SAP S/4HANA, TAS, TMR, RightAngle, and the other systems in §3.1 — resolving today’s conflicting representations into one agreed model and mapping. Full MDM execution is a later, separate decision.

4.3 Why this boundary is the right call right now

Enterprise MDM practice recognizes four implementation styles on a spectrum from “read-only, source-of-record stays put” to “the MDM hub becomes the system of record and pushes data back everywhere” (Profisee; Semarchy; Stibo Systems; Informatica).

StyleWhat it doesWrites back to sources?Relevance here
RegistryCross-references matched records across systems; stores only IDs, keys, and the matching attributes needed to link themNo — read-onlyThe pattern this phase follows. Sources keep full ownership and data quality responsibility; we analyze and link without overwriting anything.
ConsolidationCopies data into a central store for reporting/analytics without becoming the operational system of recordNoA plausible next step once the model is proven.
CoexistenceConsolidates centrally and selectively writes governed values back to sourcesYes, selectivelyWhere full MDM begins — deferred.
Centralized / TransactionalThe MDM hub becomes the authoritative system of record for all master dataYes, fullyThe eventual role of SAP MDG / Profisee — deferred.

Doing the conceptual/logical modeling and cross-system mapping first, in a Registry-style, non-invasive way, is lower-risk and faster to value than committing to Coexistence or Centralized MDM immediately: no source system needs to change, no team needs authority over systems it does not own, and the resulting model and mappings become the tested foundation Profisee/SAP MDG will need anyway when matching and golden-record work is eventually scoped. The same non-invasive philosophy underlies newer unified-profile identity-resolution approaches: a unified profile is explicitly “a set of keys that unlock your source data,” not a golden record, and identity resolution “doesn’t pick winning values or override any existing data while unifying profiles” (Salesforce Data 360 architecture).

Non-negotiable constraintNothing in this workstream modifies, cleanses, reformats, re-keys, or overwrites any value in any source system. All standardization happens in (a) the agreed models, (b) the mapping and cross-reference artifacts we own, and (c) view-time conventions. If a task seems to require changing source data, stop and escalate — it is out of scope by definition (§4.2).

Industry guidance on enterprise data modeling explicitly favors breadth over depth early — mutually exclusive objects, no more than three levels deep — before going deep (LeanIX enterprise data modeling practices).

5.2 Guiding principles — how to standardize without touching source values

These 11 principles are the contract every modeler on this workstream works under. They are the practical meaning of “standardize the Customer domain model without changing the values in individual system representations.”

#PrincipleWhat it means in practice
P1Sources are read-only to usNo writes, no cleansing in place, no re-keying, no “quick fixes” in any source system. Data-quality findings are documented and routed to the system owner, never fixed by us at the source.
P2Standardize semantics, not valuesWe standardize what “customer,” “account,” “ship-to,” and every attribute mean — definitions, roles, keys, cardinalities, allowed-value domains. Source values stay exactly as they are; equivalence is expressed through mappings, not edits.
P3One canonical hub per concept — never point-to-pointEvery system maps to the canonical model, not to other systems. A canonical data model reduces the mapping burden from n² point-to-point mappings to 2n (each system maps in and out of one shared model), improves governance, and insulates us from system change (Alation, Canonical Data Models).
P4Every disagreement becomes a dated, owned decision — never a silent overwriteConflicts go into the conflict register (§7) and are resolved in the decision log (§10) by a named owner. “Survivorship” exists only as a documented, view-time preference (which source is preferred for which attribute in which context) — never a stored overwrite (§14).
P5Model roles, not fixed typesParty/Role pattern (§3.3): a party holds time-bounded roles (Sold-to, Payer, Consignee, Exchange counterparty…). Never hard-code “customer” as a mutually exclusive subtype — the same party is routinely also a supplier, carrier, or terminal owner (§3.2).
P6Everything is effective-dated and versionedRoles, relationships, mappings, and decisions carry from/to dates and a version. Models are living assets refined iteratively, not one-off blueprints (SAP data-architecture guidance).
P7Anchor identity with source-independent identifiers where they existExternal identifiers no in-scope system “owns” — e.g., D-U-N-S numbers, LEIs, government tax IDs — are legitimate bootstrap anchors for the cross-reference schema (§14), alongside deterministic keys. They supplement, never replace, steward review.
P8Build once for Customer, design for the second domainEvery template, convention, and governance rule is written so a second domain can adopt it unchanged (§25). Domain-specific content stays in clearly marked annexes.
P9Catalog-firstEvery artifact (term, model, mapping, metadata document) is registered and linked in the enterprise catalog (Purview) as it is produced — glossary terms tied to data assets, owners named, lineage visible (§19, §20). Definitions that live in a wiki die there.
P10Design outputs to be match-ready MDM inputsAlthough matching is out of scope, every mapping and cross-reference row must carry what Profisee/MDG will need later: match attributes, confidence, method, evidence, and steward disposition (§12, §14).
P11Meaning and compute are governed separately — concepts are not KPIsDomain concepts (Party, Role, Account) live in the meaning band and never become formulas: a KPI such as Crack Spread can become a number; Customer cannot — it is a shared concept, not a KPI. KPIs are bound to concepts by ontology IRI and computed only in the semantic layer (§20.2).

6.1 Why it matters

Every later step inherits this step’s mistakes. A system missed here becomes an unmapped “customer” silo that re-emerges after the model is approved; a system included without an owner becomes a stalled dependency. The charter’s dependencies (§2.4) — domain-owner availability, source-system access, technical SMEs — are closed or confirmed as risks right here.

6.2 Inputs and prerequisites

  • The candidate system list in §3.1 (mastering, confirmed, additional, related-party-pending).
  • IDP discovery output — the six systems detailed in §3.1 were already “identified in IDP with potential customer data”; use IDP scans as the starting hypothesis, not as ground truth.
  • Existing MPC mapping inputs (dependency — locate before starting fresh; §23).
  • Domain-owner and SME contact list; access-request process per system.

6.3 How to do it

  1. Confirm the system list with domain owners. Walk the §3.1 table with each domain owner. Close every “related-party system pending confirmation” (Kittyhawk, Contract_Reporting, Nvision, EIS, Brand Credit Card Master, Corp_Vetting, OPIS, LBRS) with an explicit decision: in the Customer domain / belongs to another domain (Employee, Vendor, Supplier) / genuinely shared. Record the decision, the decider, and the date — these are governance decisions, not modeling convenience.
  2. Assign two names per system: a business owner (accountable for meaning) and a technical SME (accountable for field-level truth). No unnamed system proceeds to Step 2.
  3. Confirm read access for each in-scope system or its replica in the lakehouse (see §6.5). File access requests immediately — lead times are a common critical-path item.
  4. Identify candidate party-like objects per system. Use IDP scans, system documentation, and SME interviews to list tables/entities that plausibly carry party, account, contact, address, or relationship data. The party-entity landscape in §3.2 (e.g., DlrMaster, TMR Customer, TAS & CBS Customer, BusinessAssociate) is the worked example of the shape you are looking for.
  5. Open a Technical Metadata Document shell per system (specification in §19) and a row in the System Inventory Register per system. Register each system and its candidate objects in Purview as they are confirmed (P9).
  6. Flag scope risk early. If a system’s data turns out to be employee-only or vendor-only (e.g., LBRS appears employee-dominated in the §3.2 landscape), route it back to the domain owner for a scope decision rather than silently modeling it.

6.6 Best practices

  • Breadth before depth. Confirm all systems at shallow depth before deep-diving any single one; enterprise data-modeling guidance warns against deep early dives that create overlap and rework (LeanIX).
  • Use existing models. Several in-scope systems publish their own data models (SAP’s Business Partner model is documented in §17); familiarize yourself before inventing your own view of them (LeanIX).
  • Interview, don’t just scan. Automated discovery (IDP/Purview) finds tables; only SMEs tell you that DlrClass is a dealer classification, not a customer. Pair every scan with an SME walkthrough.
  • Profile before you rule. Data profiling across sources surfaces format inconsistencies, naming conflicts, and duplicates that define the real scope of standardization — audit/profile the estate before writing rules (Atlan standardization framework).

6.7 Pitfalls

Watch out for
  • Treating the IDP system list as complete — it finds registered systems; ask domain owners what else they use (shadow spreadsheets included).
  • Starting modeling with unnamed owners — every unnamed system becomes a §23 dependency escalation later.
  • Letting related-party systems drift into scope by silence — an undecided “pending confirmation” system at mapping time is a blocker, so force decisions in this step.

6.8 Definition of done — Step 1

  • Every system in §3.1 has an explicit in/out/shared decision recorded with decider and date.
  • Every in-scope system has a named business owner and technical SME, and access granted or requested.
  • System Inventory Register complete; Technical Metadata Document shells opened; systems and candidate objects registered in Purview.
  • Existing MPC mapping inputs located and reviewed (or their absence confirmed).

Process areaConceptual & Logical Modeling
ObjectiveAgree, with business stakeholders, one shared conceptual picture of the Customer domain — the entities, their relationships, and the business rules between them — expressed in business language, free of any system’s structure.
Primary outputsConceptual ERD (Party/Role pattern); business definitions for every entity; validation record from domain owners
Feeds deliverable(s)#1 Conceptual data model; #4 Standardized view; feeds #6 Ontology strategy (§20)
Health metric it moves# of conflicts resolved vs. open; # open definition issues pending SME input (§21)

8.1 Why it matters

The conceptual model is where “one agreed definition of Customer across every system” is actually won or lost. Logical and mapping work built on an unvalidated conceptual model amplifies disagreement instead of resolving it. The conceptual model is also the shared language between business and IT — the “what,” before the logical model’s structured “how” (SAP data-architecture guidance).

8.2 Inputs

  • Step 2’s Object Inventory, Conflict Register, current-state ERDs, glossary seeds.
  • The Party Model pattern (§3.3) and the downstream role evidence (§15–§18).
  • Business use cases — conceptual modeling starts from business use cases, not from tables (EW Solutions). Use the order-to-cash scenarios in §8.3 step 3.

8.3 How to do it

  1. Draft the core entity set from the UDM/Party pattern (translate, don’t copy — the charter’s “Domain concept perspective”). Recommended starting set for this domain: 
    PARTY (supertype: PERSON | ORGANIZATION | LEGAL ENTITY)
PARTY ROLE            — a time-bounded function a party holds (Sold-to, Payer, Consignee, …)
PARTY RELATIONSHIP    — a dated, typed link between two parties (parent-of, guarantor-of, …)
CUSTOMER ACCOUNT      — the commercial/financial relationship container a party holds with us
CONTACT POINT         — addresses, delivery locations, contacts, with usage types
EXTERNAL IDENTIFIER   — D-U-N-S, LEI, tax IDs, source system keys
AGREEMENT / CONTRACT CONTEXT — the commercial frame a role is exercised under
  2. Define each entity in plain business language, with inclusion/exclusion rules (“a Party may exist with no Customer Account”; “a Customer Account always belongs to exactly one Party”). Push every definition into the Purview glossary as a term.
  3. Stress-test against real downstream scenarios with domain owners — this is where wrong abstractions die early:
    • Exchange lift: a trading counterparty lifts product at our rack under a buy/sell agreement (TDL BOL capture) while holding no sales-area account in SAP. Which roles exist, on which entities?
    • Asphalt municipality: a public entity buys binder on a project-indexed contract (Asphalt Marketing), is tax-exempt for one product/jurisdiction but not another, and pays from a different municipal entity. Where do role, tax, and payer live?
    • Throughput storage: a JV partner owns inventory in our tanks and pays handling tariffs (Terminaling Throughputs) — a customer who never buys a molecule.
    • Branded dealer network: a dealer group with parent/child stations (DlrMaster/DlrHierarchy), loyalty participation, and brand MTD/YTD tracking in TMR.
    • Pipeline custody transfer: title passes at a FlowCal meter to a different legal entity than the consignee receiving the product.
  4. Define relationships and cardinality as business questions, then answer them with stakeholders: Can one party hold multiple customer accounts? Can one account have multiple payers over time? Can a ship-to location be shared by two accounts? Can a party be both customer and supplier (the §3.2 landscape says yes, routinely)? (Conceptual modeling process).
  5. Validate with domain owners and business stakeholders (Commercial, Finance, IT) before any logical work — walk scenarios with simple diagrams and confirm the model matches how they see operations (validate with stakeholders; involve business stakeholders). Record sign-off; unresolved disagreements go to the Conflict Register with owners and dates.
  6. Resolve Step 2 conflicts at the concept level. Each Conflict Register item should end with: mapped to a canonical concept, split into two concepts, or escalated. Metric “# conflicts resolved vs. open” moves here first.

8.4 Output artifact specification

  • Conceptual ERD — entities and named relationships only; no keys, no types. One enterprise view + per-scenario views if needed.
  • Entity definition sheet — per entity: definition, inclusion/exclusion rules, examples drawn from real systems (“a TDL exchange partner is a Party holding the Exchange-Counterparty role”), source systems where the concept appears, glossary link, owner.
  • Validation record — who validated, when, what changed as a result.

8.5 Best practices

  • Engage business stakeholders during conceptual modeling to capture real-world relationships and validate semantics — models must reflect how the business functions, not how systems store data (SAP).
  • Keep objects mutually exclusive and stable. Good enterprise data objects don’t overlap and survive reorganizations; test by checking whether lower-level objects can be assigned without ambiguity (LeanIX).
  • Stay mid-spectrum on formality. A well-structured concept model with typed relationships delivers most of the value of a formal ontology at a fraction of the cost; formal OWL/RDF only where regulation or AI consumption demands it (§20; Atlan, Ontology vs Semantic Layer).

8.6 Pitfalls

Watch out for
  • Modeling one system’s structure as “the” conceptual model (SAP’s BP model is an input, §17 — not the answer).
  • A fixed “Customer” subtype hierarchy — it breaks on the first party that is both customer and supplier (P5).
  • Starting an 18-month formal-ontology project — the classic failure mode; glossary-first, formalize later (§20).
  • Validating only with IT — the conflicts that matter live between Commercial, Finance, and Operations.

8.7 Definition of done — Step 3

  • Conceptual ERD + entity definition sheets signed off by domain owners (Commercial, Finance, IT present).
  • All five §8.3 scenarios expressible in the model without workarounds.
  • Every concept-level conflict in the register is resolved, split, or escalated with an owner.
  • Glossary terms published in Purview for every entity and key relationship.

Primary outputsLogical ERD; Canonical Attribute Dictionary; key strategy; Critical Data Element list

9.1 Why it matters

The logical model is the bridge between business agreement and technical implementation: a documented logical model with attribute definitions, relationships, cardinalities, and rules gives technical teams and business users one authoritative reference (SAP). It is also the “target” side of every source-to-target mapping in Step 5 — if the target is vague, mappings cannot be tested for completeness (metric: % source-to-target mapping completed).

9.2 Inputs

  • Signed-off conceptual model and entity definitions (Step 3).
  • Conflict Register (attribute-level conflicts get resolved here) and Object Inventory attribute semantics (Step 2).
  • Downstream considerations (§18) and SAP BP structure (§17) as reference inputs — not constraints.

9.3 How to do it (standard conceptual→logical progression)

  1. Validate the conceptual model is complete for in-scope scenarios (Step 3 exit).
  2. Add attributes per entity — only attributes the business recognizes and at least one in-scope system carries (from the Object Inventory). Each gets: business definition, data type (platform-agnostic), format/length, allowed-value domain or code set, nullability rule, and owner. This is the Canonical Attribute Dictionary (§9.4).
  3. Identify candidate keys, then choose primary keys (key selection steps):
    • CanonicalParty.party_id — surrogate, system-independent enterprise ID (never a reused source key; §14).
    • Natural/business keys recorded as alternate keys (tax ID, D-U-N-S) with match-key flags.
    • Source keys live only in the cross-reference schema (§14), never inside canonical entities.
  4. Normalize to 3NF; if something does not fit 3NF, split it into a new entity rather than forcing it (normalization guidance).
  5. Set cardinality and optionality on every relationship, then re-validate against scenarios: too many relationships into one entity usually signals a design flaw (cardinality validation).
  6. Make roles and relationships effective-dated (valid_from / valid_to) — party roles, hierarchy links, and address usages all change over time; SAP’s own BP model is time-dependent for the same reason (§17).
  7. Define allowed-value domains as governed reference data (role codes from §15, address-usage types, relationship types) — versioned, owned, published to the glossary.
  8. Flag Critical Data Elements (CDEs) — the attributes whose quality matters most (legal name, tax IDs, credit-relevant fields, match-key attributes). Focus CDEs on what is important, not on completeness across every column; CDEs carry the deep business expectations and DQ rules (Microsoft Purview Unified Catalog guidance).
  9. Validate the logical model against business requirements, then iterate — logical modeling is not one-and-done (iterate and refine).
  10. Freeze a baseline version (v0.9) as the Step 5 mapping target; changes after baseline follow the change-classification rules in §11.5.

9.5 Best practices

  • Document meticulously and consistently — definitions, cardinalities, and rules in one authoritative place (SAP).
  • Design for change: extension mechanism for new systems/roles without redefining the core (§13, object 5); the odds of nailing the model first time are slim (data modeling best practices).
  • Keep the logical model technology-agnostic — no SAP field names, no Salesforce API names in canonical entities; those live in the mappings (§10).
  • Enforce naming conventions as policy, not preference — embed them in review gates; DAMA DMBOK treats naming standards as a core governance deliverable (Atlan / DAMA).

9.6 Pitfalls

Watch out for
  • Leaking source-system codes into canonical entities (e.g., an SAP account-group value as a canonical attribute) — canonical domains are enterprise-owned (§11).
  • Modeling “tax status” or “credit limit” as simple account attributes — they are qualified by role, product, jurisdiction, and time (§18).
  • Reusing a source system’s ID as the enterprise ID — when that system merges, migrates, or is decommissioned, the enterprise ID collapses with it.
  • Attribute hoarding — an attribute no in-scope system carries and no use case needs is speculation; park it in the extension annex.

9.7 Definition of done — Step 4

  • Every attribute has a definition, type, domain, key/match usage, owner, and status; CDEs flagged.
  • All relationship cardinalities re-validated against the §8.3 scenarios.
  • Reference code sets (roles, usages, relationship types) versioned and published to Purview glossary.
  • Metric “% of attributes with agreed canonical definition” reported for the first time.

10.3 How to do it

  1. Write one source-to-target mapping document per source system using the §10.4 template. Map every party-like object/attribute to its canonical entity/attribute, in both directions of meaning: what the source value means canonically, and which canonical attributes the source can supply. Mark each attribute authoritative-source candidate or reference-only, and the role context it applies in (a TAS contact is authoritative for rack-scheduling contact, not for legal name).
  2. Agree the Source-Authority matrix (§10.5) attribute by attribute, role context by role context, with the domain owner. This is the survivorship deliverable for this phase: a documented preference, applied only when constructing a governed view — never a stored overwrite (P4).
  3. Standardize at view-time only. Canonical formats (casing, address normalization, code-set translation) are defined as transformation rules in the mapping document, applied when a standardized view is materialized (e.g., a Silver/gold view) or when match keys are computed inside our own cross-reference store. The source column itself is never reformatted (P1/P2).

Design the Enterprise ID and Cross-Reference schema (§14): one SourceObjectRegistration row per source-system object, with a slot for a later read-only snapshot of match attributes, candidate canonical party, confidence, and method. Do not populate candidate links this phase. Do not put role_code on SourceObjectRegistration. Roles go on SourceRoleAssignment (one dated row per contextual role).
  5. Record the hybrid match approach as design (deterministic rules first: exact tax-ID, D-U-N-S, or known cross-system key matches, e.g. DlrExternalNoXref, SAP CVI link tables §17; then probabilistic/fuzzy scoring on name+address). Cite entity-resolution best practice and MDM entity resolution guide. This phase does not generate or score candidates. Output of a later MDM run is candidate links with confidence and evidence, not merges.
  6. Document three confidence bands with the domain owner as the later MDM contract: auto-link (high confidence, logged), steward review (uncertain), no-link (low). A later MDM run targets a review queue of about 1 to 3 percent of candidate pairs; above about 5 percent, tune rules or blocking (data-matching guide). This phase does not auto-link and does not operate a queue.

  8. Validate by walkthrough and reconciliation: pick 10–20 real parties spanning the §8.3 scenarios; trace each from every system through the xref to its role-qualified canonical representation (§14.5 worked example). Reconcile counts per system (mapped vs. unmapped vs. legitimately out-of-scope) with the SME.

  • Map through the canonical hub only — no point-to-point system mappings (P3): each new system adds 2 mappings (to/from canonical), not n (Alation).

  • Baking a “winning value” into a persisted table — that is golden-record creation, and it is out of scope (§4.2). Preferences stay as metadata.
  • If a later MDM run has more than 5 percent of pairs in review, tune thresholds. Do not hire more stewards to absorb a bad rule set (MatchLogic). This phase has no review queue.

14.5 Worked example — one party, five systems, zero source changes

In the xref schema: five SourceObjectRegistration rows (different systems, different native IDs) all linked to one CanonicalParty (ABC Paving & Materials, LEGAL_ENTITY), plus one more registration for the parent. A reviewer can now answer “who is ABC?” — in any role, in any system — from one place.

PART C — REFERENCE STANDARDS AND MODELS18. Downstream O&G-specific modeling considerations

  • Corporate structures separate operating, contracting, and treasury functions. The entity that lifts product (TAS/FlowCal) commonly differs from the contract holder (RightAngle), the invoice recipient, and the payer (a shared treasury/finance company in SAP). Model all as separate, simultaneously valid roles — not exceptions to resolve away.
  • Title transfer is a specific point in the physical/legal chain, not a proxy for “who is the customer.” Depending on contract terms (rack, ex-terminal, FOB, delivered), the title-transfer party at a FlowCal custody-transfer event can differ from the consignee on the same movement.
  • Tax-liable party and exemption-certificate party are role-, product-, and jurisdiction-specific, not account-specific. The same legal entity may be tax-liable for one product/jurisdiction and exempt for another (dyed diesel, aviation, export). A model storing “tax status” on the account is wrong on the second scenario.
  • Credit exposure and guarantees roll up a corporate hierarchy, while delivery and consumption happen at operating-subsidiary level. Credit counterparty/guarantor is frequently the parent; sold-to/consignee the subsidiary — the hierarchy model (§13, object 3) must carry both directions.
  • Exchange (buy/sell) partners are counterparties, not buyers. TDL balances volumetric over/under-lift ledgers via BOL capture; there may be no invoice and no sales-area account. Force-fitting them into “sold-to” breaks both TDL and SAP mappings.
  • Throughput/storage customers never buy product. They own inventory in our tanks and pay fee-based tariffs (Terminaling Throughputs). The role is storage account holder; the commodity sale concepts do not apply.
  • Branded dealer networks are hierarchies with loyalty and brand programs attached (DlrMaster, DlrHierarchy, DlrMasterLoyalty) — stations roll up to dealer groups; brand MTD/YTD measures (TMR) attach at multiple levels.
  • Specialized channels need first-class role variants. Asphalt’s public-sector project-indexed contracts and MSMIS’s marine/regulatory context do not fit a generic “customer” shape — treat them as Tier-3 roles (§15), not later exceptions.

20.1 The boundary we will respect: ontology vs. semantic layer

One gives structure; the other gives numbers — enterprises need both, connected by a context layer (the catalog) that links definitions to data assets and lineage (Atlan, Ontology vs Semantic Layer; Cube semantic-layer deep dive).

20.3 The phased strategy we propose

  1. Phase A — Governed glossary (now, part of Steps 2–4): flat → hierarchical glossary in Purview; every canonical concept is a term with definition, synonyms (system names), owner, and linked data assets. A well-structured glossary delivers most ontological value at a fraction of the cost (Atlan).
  2. Phase B — Concept model with typed relationships (= our conceptual model, §8): the Party/Role/Relationship structure doubles as the lightweight ontology. No separate ontology build at this stage — that is the point of reusing the conceptual model. When a module is serialized, it is stored as Turtle files in git with one stable, namespaced IRI per concept — those IRIs are the binding keys the KPI architecture relies on (§20.2).
  3. Phase C — KPI/concept linkage: connect Customer concepts to the KPIs that reference them (e.g., Wholesale MTD/YTD from TMR; rack pricing KPIs feeding off RightAngle + OPIS) so every metric’s entity references resolve to governed definitions. Each certified KPI gets exactly one catalog row — name, owner, formula pointer, used-in, ontology IRI — bound to exactly one semantic-layer object; variants (e.g., Wholesale MTD by consignee vs. by sold-to) bind to role-qualified concepts, never to the bare “Customer” concept (1:1 gate, §20.2).
  4. Phase D - Formal ontology only where justified: serialize as Turtle in git. That Turtle is the machine SoT. FOUND-003 stays the human-readable semantic model. FOUND-004 stays the ontology design and uses conceptual names. Fuseki is a demo SPARQL/SHACL runtime, not a second SoT. Scoped, incremental, and justified per use case.

20.4 Failure modes this strategy avoids

Known failure modes (from industry experience)
  • Big-bang formal ontology: 18 months modeling every entity, producing an artifact disconnected from any tool anyone uses. We start glossary-light instead (Atlan).
  • Metrics before entities: semantic-layer metrics built on un-agreed entity definitions faithfully calculate inconsistent numbers. We define Customer concepts first (Steps 3–4), then connect KPIs.
  • Definitions in a wiki: meaning that is not linked to data assets and owners decays. Everything registers in Purview (P9).

20.5 Why AI consumers raise the stakes

When an LLM answers questions against enterprise data, it needs to know precisely what “customer” means — which entities, which roles, which assets — or it will invent joins and confuse definitions. The governed concept model + catalog linkage is what prevents hallucinated business logic (Atlan; Cube). This is a concrete future consumer of this workstream’s outputs and should be named in the strategy deliverable. The KPI architecture formalizes the pattern as two doors, both guarded: an agent asks the graph of meaning first, then the catalog, then submits only the certified measure object on the pointed semantic-layer object — it never writes a formula, and the compiled SQL is the audit trail (§20.2).

PART C — REFERENCE STANDARDS AND MODELS27. Glossary

TermDefinition
PartyA person, organization, or legal entity capable of holding rights, obligations, or relationships.
Party RoleA context-specific, time-bounded function a party performs (e.g., Payer, Consignee, Guarantor, Exchange counterparty).
Party RelationshipA dated, typed link between two parties (parent-of, guarantor-of, contact-for).
Canonical (data) modelA common, standardized representation of a domain’s concepts that each system maps to, replacing n² point-to-point mappings with 2n hub mappings.
Cross-Reference / CrosswalkA governed, read-only mapping between a source-system object and a canonical Party/Role representation.
Registry-style MDMAn MDM pattern that links and cross-references source records without copying, overwriting, or becoming their system of record.
Golden recordA single, persisted, merged “best” version of an entity’s attributes — explicitly out of scope for this phase.
SurvivorshipThe rule determining which source’s value is used for an attribute in a given context. In this phase: a documented, view-time preference only — never a stored overwrite.
Entity resolution / matchingDetermining whether records in different systems refer to the same real-world party, via deterministic rules, probabilistic scoring, or both, with steward review of uncertain cases.
Critical Data Element (CDE)An attribute whose quality is most consequential for the domain; carries explicit DQ rules and controls.
Business Partner (SAP)S/4HANA’s leading master-data object for parties; customers and vendors exist as BP roles synchronized via CVI (§17).
Partner function (SAP SD)A SAP SD partner function on a sales transaction. Sold-to, Bill-to, and Payer are party roles. Ship-to is a location, not a party role.
CVI (Customer-Vendor Integration)SAP’s synchronization mechanism keeping Business Partner and classic customer/vendor tables aligned; its link tables are ready-made cross-references.
UDMUniversal Data Model — reusable, industry-agnostic conceptual/logical data model patterns (e.g., the Party Model) that this workstream translates for the Customer and second domains.
Ontology (domain)The governed conceptual model of business meaning: what domain concepts are and how they relate (§20).
Semantic layerThe executable contract for metrics: how governed analytical values are computed and served (§20). Complements, never replaces, the ontology.
Context layer / catalogPurview is the enterprise catalog (glossary, stewardship, data-product context). Unity Catalog is the Databricks technical catalog. Bigeye is quality and cross-platform lineage. None of those three is the KPI Store catalog (§20).
IDPThe enterprise data platform/discovery capability that surfaced candidate customer-data systems (§3.1).
Bronze / Silver / GoldMedallion lakehouse layers: Bronze = raw source replicas; Silver = cleaned, named, business-meaningful dimensions (§6.5); Gold = published KPI values at agreed grains (§20.2).
Exchange (buy/sell) partnerA counterparty in product-exchange agreements, balanced volumetrically via BOLs rather than invoiced sales (TDL).
Throughput / storage customerA party owning inventory in our terminals and paying fee-based storage/handling tariffs without buying commodity.
Title-transfer partyThe party to which ownership or risk transfers at the contractually defined point (rack, terminal, FOB, delivered).
ConsigneeThe party named to receive product in a transportation or custody-transfer context — not automatically the ship-to or title-transfer party.
Branded dealer / jobberA reseller party in the branded distribution network, typically hierarchically organized (dealer group → stations) with brand/loyalty programs attached.
KPI Store / KPI architectureThe target-state pattern that separates meaning (domain understanding, ontology modules, graph of meaning) from compute (prepare, compile, consume), bound together by the ontology IRI (§20.2).
Named KPIA KPI name governed as an ontology artifact and bound to concepts by IRI; a proposed name must exist before certification can begin.
1:1 gateThe rule that a KPI can become a number but a concept cannot: one certified catalog row binds to exactly one semantic-layer object via one ontology IRI (§20.2).
Ontology IRIThe stable, namespaced identifier of a concept — the binding key between the meaning band and the compute band (one catalog row ↔ one semantic-layer object).
Candidate KPI RegisterThe proposed, not-yet-approved list of KPIs; entries are promoted to certified catalog rows only after steward certification (§20.2).
