Integrated Architecture, Workstreams, and Platform Model

This handbook shows how the primary architecture chain, architecture families, delivery workstreams, implementation pillars, and enterprise platforms fit together as one governed system.

5. Architecture families

Each architecture family is a coherent discipline with its own concepts, relationships, decisions, artifacts, and implementation responsibilities. Together they describe the full Enterprise Performance Model from business intent to governed consumption.

How to read this section: the numbered items under each family are the principal concepts or layers that belong to that architecture family. They are not always a simple parent-child hierarchy; some are related through many-to-many or overlay relationships.

5.1 Business Architecture

Purpose: describes how the enterprise creates, delivers, and protects value through domains, value streams, capabilities, work, and decisions.

  1. Enterprise — the organization as a whole, including its strategic direction, operating boundaries, and stakeholders.
  2. Business Domain — a durable area of business responsibility, expertise, language, data, and decision-making, such as Commercial, Refining, Midstream, or Corporate.
  3. Value Stream — the end-to-end flow from a trigger or opportunity to a realized stakeholder outcome.
  4. Value-Stream Stage — a major outcome-oriented step in the value stream.
  5. Capability — an enduring ability the organization must possess.
  6. Sub-capability — a more specific capability decomposed beneath a broader capability.
  7. Business Process — an ordered flow of work that realizes one or more capabilities.
  8. Business Activity — a discrete unit of work performed by a person, team, system, or combination of them.
  9. Business Decision — a choice among alternatives that changes action, resource allocation, exposure, or outcome.
  10. Business Event — something that occurs and triggers, changes, or records business activity, such as an order receipt, refinery outage, nomination rejection, or price publication.
  11. Business Outcome — the operational, financial, customer, risk, or strategic result produced by the business architecture.
  12. Role and Organization — accountable actors, teams, or organizational units that own capabilities, processes, activities, decisions, and outcomes.

5.2 Performance Architecture

Purpose: defines how performance is observed, interpreted, explained, evaluated, governed, and acted upon.

  1. Strategic Objective — a result the enterprise intends to achieve.
  2. Performance Outcome — an observable result related to the objective.
  3. Performance Driver — a condition or factor that influences an outcome or KPI.
  4. Measurement — an observed or calculated value describing an event, object, condition, or result.
  5. Raw Measurement — a value directly recorded from a source event or system.
  6. Derived Measurement — a value calculated from one or more measurements.
  7. Metric — a standardized, repeatable measure used to describe, compare, monitor, or diagnose performance.
  8. Operational Metric — a measure of routine operating performance.
  9. Diagnostic Metric — a measure used to explain why another metric or KPI changed.
  10. Performance Indicator — an important monitored measure that may not yet be governed as an enterprise KPI.
  11. Candidate KPI — a proposed performance indicator undergoing governance review.
  12. Enterprise KPI — a governed metric used to evaluate an objective or critical outcome and trigger accountability or action.
  13. Target — the desired value or expected condition.
  14. Threshold — warning, critical, or other evaluation boundary used to determine status.
  15. Status — the evaluated condition of the KPI, such as On Target, Warning, or Critical.
  16. Performance Variance — the difference between actual and target, plan, budget, forecast, benchmark, or prior period.
  17. Performance Action — the intervention, escalation, or management response triggered by performance.

5.3 Semantic Architecture

Purpose: establishes the authoritative system of business meaning shared by people, metadata platforms, data products, analytical models, APIs, and AI.

  1. Business Concept — a meaningful type of thing in the enterprise, such as Customer, Trade, Shipment, KPI, or Capability.
  2. Concept Definition — the authoritative written meaning of the concept.
  3. Preferred Term — the approved business name for the concept.
  4. Synonym and Alias — alternate names linked to the preferred term.
  5. Classification — the category to which a concept belongs, such as Metric, KPI, Foundational Data Product, or Business Process.
  6. Relationship Type — the defined semantic connection between concepts, such as measures, supplies, realizes, or consumes.
  7. Business Rule — a statement governing valid meaning, behavior, calculation, or classification.
  8. Constraint — a condition that must hold, such as required ownership or cardinality.
  9. Hierarchy — a parent-child or broader-narrower structure among concepts.
  10. Scope — the business, geography, product, legal entity, or organizational boundary within which the meaning applies.
  11. Context — the conditions that qualify interpretation, including grain, time, unit, dimensions, or process stage.
  12. Identifier — the stable unique identity of a semantic concept across implementations.
  13. Version and Effective Period — the time-bounded version of the approved meaning.
  14. Ownership and Stewardship — accountability for definition, quality, interpretation, and change.
  15. Semantic Mapping — the link from the authoritative concept to Purview terms, ontology classes, SQL fields, KPI metadata, Power BI measures, APIs, and AI context.

5.4 Ontology Architecture

Purpose: formalizes the approved semantic model into a machine-readable representation that supports validation, querying, graph construction, and reasoning.

  1. Ontology Namespace — the controlled identity space for ontology resources.
  2. Class — a formal category of things, such as EnterpriseKPI, BusinessProcess, or DataProduct.
  3. Subclass — a more specialized class inheriting the meaning of a broader class.
  4. Individual or Instance — a specific enterprise object, such as Gasoline Netback CPG or Order-to-Cash.
  5. Object Property — a formal relationship between instances, such as evaluatesObjective or suppliedBy.
  6. Data Property — a property holding a literal value, such as targetValue, timeGrain, or effectiveFrom.
  7. Domain and Range — constraints specifying which classes a property connects.
  8. Cardinality — minimum, maximum, or exact relationship counts.
  9. Inheritance — the propagation of class meaning and constraints to subclasses.
  10. Axiom — a formal statement that is logically asserted in the ontology.
  11. Controlled Vocabulary — an approved set of values, statuses, types, or relationship names.
  12. SHACL Shape — a validation structure used to test whether graph data meets governance rules.
  13. Knowledge Graph — the populated network of ontology-governed instances and relationships.
  14. Competency Question — a business question the ontology and knowledge graph should be able to answer.
  15. Ontology Mapping — links from ontology resources to Purview terms, ER/Studio entities, Unity Catalog assets, KPI metadata, and Power BI objects.

5.5 Data Architecture

Purpose: defines how data is sourced, ingested, standardized, integrated, stored, protected, governed, traced, and made available for reuse.

  1. Source System — an operational, market, financial, technical, or external system that originates data.
  2. Source Data Contract — expectations governing source structure, delivery, semantics, and service behavior.
  3. Ingestion — movement of data from source into the platform.
  4. Raw / Bronze Layer — source-oriented preserved data supporting auditability, replay, and lineage.
  5. Silver Layer — cleaned, standardized, conformed, quality-controlled business facts and dimensions.
  6. Gold Layer — integrated, reusable, decision-ready domain and analytical structures.
  7. KPI Store — governed persistence of official KPI values and evaluation metadata.
  8. Semantic / Consumption Layer — approved views and interfaces available to consumers.
  9. Data Asset — a table, view, file, stream, model, or other governed technical object.
  10. Data Model — the conceptual, logical, or physical structure of data.
  11. Master and Reference Data — shared identities, hierarchies, codes, and classifications.
  12. Data Lineage — traceability from source through transformation to consumption.
  13. Data Quality — fitness for purpose across completeness, accuracy, consistency, timeliness, validity, and uniqueness.
  14. Security and Access Control — policies and mechanisms controlling data use.
  15. Retention and Lifecycle — rules for preservation, archival, supersession, and retirement.
  16. Metadata — descriptive, technical, operational, semantic, governance, and lineage information about data.

5.6 Data-Product Architecture

Purpose: packages trusted data and reusable business logic into managed products with clear ownership, contracts, quality, service levels, and consumers.

  1. Data Product — a managed reusable asset designed for defined consumers and use cases.
  2. Foundational Data Product — a trusted reusable representation of core entities, events, or transactions.
  3. Derived Data Product — a decision-ready product combining foundational products and higher-order business logic.
  4. Product Purpose — the business problem or decision the product serves.
  5. Business Owner — accountability for business meaning, priority, and value.
  6. Data Product Owner — accountability for product delivery, lifecycle, and service.
  7. Consumer — a person, team, report, model, application, API, workflow, or agent using the product.
  8. Product Interface — SQL view, table, API, event stream, file, model endpoint, or semantic model through which the product is consumed.
  9. Data Contract — agreed structure, meaning, quality, service level, and change behavior.
  10. Grain and Keys — the unit of observation and identity structure.
  11. Quality Rules — product-specific checks and acceptance conditions.
  12. Service Level Objective — freshness, availability, latency, recovery, and support expectations.
  13. Security Classification — rules governing permitted use and access.
  14. Lineage and Dependencies — source products, transformations, downstream consumers, and KPI dependencies.
  15. Adoption and Value Measures — indicators showing product use, reuse, reliability, and business benefit.
  16. Lifecycle Status — proposed, active, certified, deprecated, superseded, or retired.

5.7 Consumption Architecture

Purpose: defines how governed data and meaning are securely presented to users, applications, analytical tools, automation, and AI.

  1. Consumer — a person, team, report, application, API, workflow, or AI agent.
  2. Consumption Use Case — the decision, analysis, monitoring, action, or experience being supported.
  3. Semantic SQL View — a stable governed SQL contract exposing approved business data.
  4. Power BI Semantic Model — an analytical implementation containing tables, relationships, measures, hierarchies, formatting, and security.
  5. Report and Dashboard — a visual analytical experience serving defined users and decisions.
  6. Scorecard — a focused view of KPIs, targets, status, trends, and accountability.
  7. API — a programmatic interface exposing governed data or performance information.
  8. Data Service — a reusable service providing data, calculations, or domain logic.
  9. Alert — notification generated by an event, anomaly, or threshold condition.
  10. Automation Workflow — an orchestrated response triggered by data or performance conditions.
  11. Analytical Model — statistical, optimization, forecasting, or machine-learning model consuming governed data.
  12. AI Application or Agent — an AI-enabled consumer using governed semantic and data context.
  13. Security Context — row-level, object-level, attribute-level, or identity-aware access behavior.
  14. Presentation Logic — visual or interaction-specific behavior that does not redefine core business logic.
  15. Usage Telemetry — evidence about adoption, performance, consumer behavior, and value.

5.8 Integration Architecture

Purpose: connects systems, metadata, models, lineage, quality, governance, and implementations so that the EPM remains consistent across platforms.

  1. Integration Endpoint — a source or target participating in metadata or data exchange.
  2. Integration Pattern — batch, event-driven, API, file-based, database, message, or metadata synchronization approach.
  3. Metadata Exchange — movement or synchronization of technical, semantic, governance, quality, and lineage metadata.
  4. Semantic Synchronization — alignment of definitions, identifiers, classifications, and relationships across systems.
  5. Model Synchronization — alignment among conceptual, logical, physical, ontology, and platform models.
  6. Lineage Synchronization — connection of source, transformation, data-product, KPI, semantic-view, and report lineage.
  7. Quality Synchronization — exchange of quality rules, results, incidents, and product-health status.
  8. Ownership Synchronization — alignment of business owner, data owner, product owner, and steward information.
  9. Certification Synchronization — propagation of approved, certified, deprecated, or retired status.
  10. Identity Mapping — reconciliation of object identifiers across Purview, ER/Studio, Unity Catalog, Bigeye, KPI metadata, Power BI, and ontology resources.
  11. Transformation and Orchestration — governed movement, generation, and sequencing of data and metadata.
  12. API and Event Contract — technical agreement governing message structure, semantics, versioning, and failure behavior.
  13. Error and Exception Handling — detection, routing, retry, reconciliation, and issue ownership.
  14. Observability — monitoring of integration freshness, latency, failures, volume, and health.
  15. Automation — metadata-driven generation of SQL, views, registrations, lineage, quality checks, and graph updates.

5.9 Governance Architecture

Purpose: establishes the decision rights, policies, controls, accountability, and lifecycle management required to keep the EPM trusted and coherent.

  1. Governance Scope — the domains, concepts, assets, products, metrics, KPIs, systems, and consumers subject to governance.
  2. Policy — a mandatory principle or rule governing behavior.
  3. Standard — an approved method, format, naming rule, or implementation expectation.
  4. Business Owner — accountability for business definition, outcome, or KPI.
  5. Data Owner — accountability for data within a defined scope.
  6. Data Steward — operational responsibility for definition, quality, issue resolution, and metadata.
  7. Data Product Owner — accountability for product value, delivery, service, and lifecycle.
  8. Decision Right — authority to approve, reject, change, certify, or retire an item.
  9. Approval Workflow — the sequence of review and authorization steps.
  10. Certification — formal confirmation that a concept, KPI, product, or consumer interface is approved for use.
  11. Quality Control — checks, thresholds, monitoring, and issue management for trusted use.
  12. Lineage Control — assurance that origin, transformation, and consumption are traceable.
  13. Security and Privacy Control — governance over access, sensitivity, retention, and permitted use.
  14. Version and Effective Dating — controlled management of changes over time.
  15. Issue and Exception Management — identification, ownership, escalation, remediation, and waiver handling.
  16. Change Control — assessment and approval of changes to definitions, models, formulas, data products, and interfaces.
  17. Lifecycle Management — proposal, draft, approval, active use, deprecation, supersession, and retirement.
  18. Audit Evidence — records demonstrating compliance, approval, quality, lineage, and implementation state.

Feedback loop: System Integration reports technical lineage, data quality, and implementation reality. KPI Store and EPM reveal missing or unreliable measurements. Domain Modeling then clarifies or adjusts concepts and relationships. The approved changes flow back into integrated implementations.

15. What Tableau-to-Power BI Measurement Discovery contributes

This workstream is not simply a technical XML extraction exercise. It is the evidence-gathering and reconciliation mechanism that helps build the Measurement Catalog, KPI Catalog, semantic model, data-product requirements, and retirement decisions.

Its specific contributions

Legacy logic inventory

Extracts calculated fields, parameters, filters, dependencies, worksheets, and workbook usage from Tableau XML.

Formula normalization

Standardizes formula text so equivalent or near-equivalent logic can be compared.

Duplicate detection

Finds multiple names or implementations of the same measure across reports and teams.

Power BI reconciliation

Compares historical Tableau logic with the current Power BI implementation to identify migrated changes, omissions, and drift.

Business-context mapping

Maps calculations to domain, value stream, stage, capability, process, decision, objective, data product, and source system.

Measurement classification

Classifies each item as raw measurement, derived measurement, operational metric, diagnostic metric, performance indicator, candidate KPI, benchmark, helper, duplicate, or retired item.

KPI candidate discovery

Surfaces measures that may warrant governance review but does not automatically promote them to KPIs.

Semantic-model evidence

Reveals business terminology, repeated concepts, calculation dependencies, grain assumptions, and inconsistent definitions.

Data-product requirements

Shows which reusable facts and calculations should move from reports into foundational or derived data products.

Retirement and consolidation

Supports explicit decisions to merge, standardize, retain locally, replace, or retire calculations.
